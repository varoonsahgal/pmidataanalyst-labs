Understand it · 1 minute

The point

Every session today has one sentence at its centre. This is Session 1's. Every section below ends with a "Back to the point" box that ties what you just read back to it.

The sentence the session proves

An AI model can't read your mind. Whatever you leave out of a question, it fills in with a confident guess, and you never see the guess.

So the analyst's job is to say the missing things out loud (which tables, which dates, what "adoption" means, who we're allowed to count) and then check the answer against the real data.

Here's what happens in the lab, in plain words. A manager in Indonesia asks a six-word question: "What's our early VEEV adoption looking like?" Students paste that question into Claude (an AI chat app). Claude can't answer it properly, because the question leaves out almost everything that matters. So students add the missing pieces one at a time, and each time they run Claude's database query to see the real number. Three surprises happen along the way:

ERRORStep 1

Asked to "just guess", Claude writes an expert-looking query against tables that don't exist.

9Step 2

Nine people under 25 (whom PMI may not market to) were hiding inside a believable count of 63.

0 rowsStep 3

One reasonable extra line makes the query return nobody at all. No error, no warning.

Step 4 turns everything students learned into a reusable prompt template, so next time the missing pieces can't be forgotten.

The class has one rule for the whole day, and Session 1 is where it starts: AI answers. Data decides. You sign. The model writes the query; the warehouse (the database) decides whether the number is right; and the analyst's name goes on the result.

Back to the point

If you remember one thing: a fluent answer is not a correct answer. The model fills every gap in your question with a guess. Everything else in this guide is detail that serves that sentence.

Understand it · 3 minutes

The story, and the business words

The lab is a small workplace story in four panels. First the panels, then every company and marketing word in them, decoded.

The ask

PMI's Indonesia Markets Lead (the manager for that country) sends a one-line Teams message: "What's our early VEEV adoption looking like?"

The shortcut

The analyst pastes it straight into Claude. Claude asks questions back. Pushed to "just guess", it writes a query against tables it made up.

The catch

Even with the real tables, the question hides two business rules (no marketing to under-25s, only people who gave permission) and a trap about blank values.

The fix

Say every rule out loud in the prompt, check each answer in the database, then save the prompt as a template for the next person.

The business words, decoded

PMI the company

Philip Morris International, the tobacco company behind Marlboro. Your students are PMI data analysts. PMI's strategy is to move adult smokers off cigarettes and onto "smoke-free" products.

Background only. All the data is fictional ("Project HORIZON") and safe for training.

IQOS · VEEV · ZYN the three products

IQOS heats tobacco instead of burning it (a device). VEEV is an e-cigarette, a vape (also a device). ZYN is a nicotine pouch you put under your lip (no device). These are the values in the product_code column.

VEEV only. PMI really did launch VEEV in Indonesia in August 2026, so this is a real question this quarter.

Market · market code a country

PMI calls each country a "market" and gives it a two-letter code: ID Indonesia, JP Japan, IT Italy. A "Markets Lead" is the manager responsible for one country.

Indonesia (market_code = 'ID'). It's a new, small market, so the numbers are small (dozens of people a month).

The lifecycle · the funnel a customer's journey

Five stages a consumer can reach, in order: registration (signs up) → trial (tries the product) → conversion (switches to it for real) → then retention (keeps using it) or churn (gives up). Marketers call this a funnel because fewer people reach each stage.

a gym: sign up for a free pass, try a class, buy a membership, keep coming or cancel.

Adoption the metric

A loose word meaning "people starting to use the product". Different teams mean different things by it: trying it once, buying it, or buying it twice. That's exactly why the model can't guess it.

The lab defines it as distinct consumers who reached trial or conversion. "Distinct" means each person counts once, however many times they show up.

Consent · marketing permission may we contact them?

A person's yes or no to receiving marketing, recorded per channel (email, SMS, push notification, post). In the database it's the true/false column marketing_allowed.

a signed permission slip. One person can have several slips, one per channel.

Age-gate · the under-25 rule who we may not market to

In HORIZON's rules, nobody under 25 may be marketed to in Indonesia or Italy. Japan is different (Session 2 shows this). The age group is stored as a band such as '18-24'.

The grounded prompt says "exclude age_band '18-24'". Leave it out and 9 under-25s end up in a marketing number.

Campaign · attribution did marketing cause it?

A campaign is a marketing push (an email burst, a retail promotion). An event is attributed to a campaign when it's tagged with that campaign's id. An event with no campaign has a blank campaign_id.

The manager asks to leave out "the 2024 VEEV launch campaign, campaign 4". No Indonesia event is tagged to any campaign, so the right answer is "nothing changes".

Q3 2025 · a quarter a three-month window

Companies split the year into four quarters. Q3 is July, August and September. The HORIZON data runs from 1 January 2024 to 31 December 2025.

The question never says "when", so the prompt has to: Q3 2025.

BPOM Indonesia's regulator

Indonesia's food and drug authority, which regulates nicotine products there. You'll see it named in the student guide; you don't need to know more.

Only as background for why the under-25 rule exists.

Back to the point

The manager's six words hide a timeframe, a definition of "adoption", two legal rules and a question about blank values. None of that is in the question, so the model has to guess all of it. What you don't say, it makes up.

Understand it · 3 minutes

The tech words

Two kinds: database words (what the queries do) and AI words (why the model behaves the way it does). Each comes with an everyday picture. If you already know one, skip it.

Database and SQL words

Database · table · warehouse where data lives

A database is a collection of tables. A table is like a spreadsheet tab: named columns across the top, one record per row. A "warehouse" is just a big database built for analysis.

The warehouse is called HORIZON (it runs on PostgreSQL, "Postgres"). Table names have two parts, like clr.consumers: folder clr, table consumers.

Schema a folder of tables

The first part of a table name. HORIZON's folders are named after data sources: clr (consumer records), mpl (marketing permissions), ref (reference lists). "Schema" also loosely means "the list of tables and columns".

Step 1 gives Claude only the folder names. It invents the tables inside them.

Row · "one row = …" the grain

The most useful sentence about any table is "one row means one ___". Analysts call that the grain. In clr.consumers one row = one person. In clr.lifecycle_events one row = one thing a person did on one day.

One person can have several event rows. That's why the queries say count(DISTINCT consumer_id).

SQL · query asking the database

SQL is the language for asking a database questions. One question is a query. It reads almost like English: SELECT these columns FROM this table WHERE this is true.

Claude writes the SQL; students copy it into SQLTools (inside VS Code), click in it and press Ctrl+E Ctrl+E to run it.

WHERE a filter

The part of a query that decides which rows to keep. Each condition is checked row by row; a row survives only if every condition comes out true.

a bouncer with a checklist. "Only true" matters: Step 3 is about a row where the answer is neither true nor false.

JOIN gluing two tables together

Puts rows from two tables side by side wherever a column matches (here, the same consumer_id). The catch: a row is glued to every match. One event matched to four permission slips becomes four rows.

The grounded query joins events to people (for the age band) and to permission slips (for consent).

count(DISTINCT …) how many different

count(*) counts rows. count(DISTINCT consumer_id) counts different people, ignoring repeats. After a join has copied rows, only DISTINCT gives the right head count.

Without DISTINCT, Step 2 would say 37 · 32 · 52 instead of 17 · 16 · 25.

GROUP BY · date_trunc one line per month

GROUP BY makes one result line per group instead of one per row. date_trunc('month', event_date) rounds every date down to the first of its month, so July 16 and July 29 both become 2025-07-01.

That's why results come back as three lines: 2025-07-01, 2025-08-01, 2025-09-01.

Half-open date range a clean "from … up to"

event_date >= '2025-07-01' AND event_date < '2025-10-01': on or after 1 July, strictly before 1 October. That's the whole quarter with no "is the 30th included?" debate.

The grounded prompt spells it out. A relative window like "the last 90 days" returns nothing, because the data ends in 2025.

NULL a blank, meaning "unknown"

An empty cell. Not zero, not false: unknown. SQL treats any comparison with a blank as unknown too, and WHERE only keeps rows that are true. So a filter like campaign_id <> 4 silently drops every blank row.

Every Indonesia event has a blank campaign id. That's the silent zero in Step 3.

<> and IS DISTINCT FROM "is not"

<> (or !=) means "is not equal to", but it answers "unknown" for blanks. IS DISTINCT FROM means the same thing and treats a blank as an ordinary value, so blank rows are kept.

The fix in Step 3: campaign_id IS DISTINCT FROM 4.

Error vs wrong answer the key distinction

An error (red text, "relation does not exist") means the database refused to run the query. A wrong answer runs fine and returns a believable number. Errors are easy to spot; wrong answers aren't.

Step 1 errors (good: you notice). Step 3 runs and returns zero rows (bad: nobody notices).

AI words

LLM · next token what Claude actually does

A large language model writes by predicting the next small piece of text (a token, roughly ¾ of a word), adding it, and repeating. It continues text that sounds likely. It doesn't look anything up or check anything.

The opener shows this live in the Transformer Explainer website.

Context window everything it can see

The model only knows what's in the conversation right now: your prompt and the chat so far. It has never seen HORIZON. If something isn't in the prompt, it's a guess.

Step 2 opens a new chat so Step 1's guesses don't leak into the window.

Temperature · sampling why answers differ

The model picks its next word from a spread of likely options, with a bit of randomness ("temperature"). Same prompt, different people, slightly different SQL. That's normal.

Students' queries won't match each other or the guide word for word. Judge by the number it returns.

Hallucination confident and false

A fluent, specific statement that isn't true: a table name that doesn't exist, a statistic nobody measured. Models do it because they're trained to produce an answer, and guessing usually scores better than saying "I don't know".

Step 1's invented tables, like clr.consumer_lifecycle.

Grounding giving it the facts

Putting the real facts in the prompt (table names, columns, definitions, rules) so the model writes against reality instead of a guess.

Step 2's prompt is a grounded prompt.

Template · chain-of-thought · few-shot three prompt patterns

Template: a prompt with fill-in-the-blank PLACEHOLDERS. Chain-of-thought: ask it to list its plan before writing SQL. Few-shot: show it one finished example so it copies the style.

Step 4 builds one template that uses all three.

The four moves students repeat all day

Claude Desktop is a chat window. It cannot see the database and cannot run anything. So every step is the same loop, and the human carries the SQL across:

Pastea prompt from the student guide into Claude Desktop.
Copythe SQL Claude writes: hover the code block, click Copy.
Runit in a SQLTools tab in VS Code: Ctrl+E Ctrl+E.
Read the numberThe database decides whether the SQL was right, not the chat.
Back to the point

Notice where the checking happens: not in Claude (it can't see the data) and not in the database (it happily runs a wrong query). The only one who can compare the answer with reality is the person in the middle. The model fills the gaps; you check what it filled them with.

The data isn't broken anywhere. The danger is in the gaps of the question: which stages count, which months, which people. Every gap you leave, the model fills with a guess, and each guess changes the number.

Understand it · 2 minutes

The three traps, drawn

The lab springs three traps in a row. If you can explain these three, you can teach the session.

Trap 1 · Fluent is not grounded (Step 1)

What Claude invents when you say "just guess"

clr.consumer_lifecycle, mpl.consent, ref.date, a column called adoption_flag… The right folder names, made-up tables inside them.

What actually exists

clr.consumers, clr.lifecycle_events, mpl.consent_records, plus four ref lists (age bands, campaigns, markets, products).

The query looks expert, with neat formatting and advanced features, and fails with ERROR: relation "…" does not exist ("relation" is database-speak for "table"). The lesson: an error is a gift. It tells you something's wrong. The scary queries are the ones that run.

Trap 2 · The rules nobody said (Step 2)

Ignore the business rules and 63 people adopted VEEV in Indonesia in Q3. Apply the under-25 rule and 54 are left. The 9 missing people are under 25: one adopter in seven. Click the button to see it:

See it63 adopters, one dot eachorange = under 25
63people counted 9 under-25s gone: one adopter in seven.

The point isn't the size of the gap. 63 looks exactly as believable as 54. Nobody would spot it by looking. Only a rule written into the prompt removes them.

A detail worth knowing

In Indonesia the two rules (age-gate and consent) remove exactly the same 9 people, because no under-25 Indonesian has a "true" permission slip. So a draft that applies only one of the two rules still gets 17 · 16 · 25. Don't let that confuse you if a student says "I forgot the consent rule and still got the right answer". Their answer is right by luck; in another market it wouldn't be.

Trap 3 · The silent zero (Step 3)

The manager adds: "also leave out the 2024 VEEV launch campaign, campaign 4." Most drafts add one line, AND le.campaign_id <> 4 ("campaign is not 4"). It looks perfect. Here's what the database does with it, row by row:

the filter campaign_id <> 4, checked one row at a time
campaign_id on the row"is it not 4?"does WHERE keep it?in plain English

Every one of Indonesia's 2,343 events is in that red row. So the filter throws away all of them, and the query returns 0 rows. A dashboard built on it would say nobody adopted VEEV in Indonesia.

Back to the point

Trap 3 is the session's sentence at full strength. "Exclude campaign 4" sounded complete, but it left one thing unsaid: what to do with events that have no campaign. The model filled that silence with a guess, and the guess deleted everyone. What you don't say, it makes up.

Walk the lab · 1 minute · class time 0:12 · slides 14 and 15

Opener: how the AI works

Before the lab, six minutes on what Claude actually is. It explains everything that happens in Step 1.

You do

On slide 14 the deck has a live next-word widget for the Icebreaker 1 sentence ("IQOS conversions in Italy last quarter were ___"). Click Next word three times and read the words aloud. Drag temperature to 0.1 (same word every time), then to 2.0 and click Ask it 10 times. Point at the dashed "which quarter?" row, then press R. Optional, live: the Transformer Explainer (a real GPT-2 in the browser) with The analyst ran the query and the result was.

Students do

Predict in the chat: "Which word will it pick?" Then compare with what the room typed at 0:05.

They see

Plausible words, picked by weight, and a different spread every time at high temperature. The one answer a good analyst gives, "which quarter?", is never on the list. (In the Transformer Explainer the top candidates are small words like "a" or "the", not a number.)

It means

The model isn't computing a result. It's continuing a sentence with whatever sounds likely. It has no idea what query the analyst ran. It writes what sounds right, not what it has checked.

You say

"It doesn't look things up. It continues text. Everything it knows about HORIZON is whatever you paste in."

Then slide 15, the four facts, one sentence each:

  1. It predicts the next token. Plausible is its job; correct is yours.
  2. The window is all it knows. If it isn't in the prompt, it's a guess.
  3. It samples. Same prompt, different answers, so nobody's SQL will match their neighbour's.
  4. It answers anyway. AI tests reward a lucky guess over "I don't know", so models learn to guess confidently. Give it a way to say "I'm not sure" (the grounded prompt does).

Optional, 30 seconds: paste clr.lifecycle_events into Tiktokenizer to show a table name splits into several tokens. The model never sees your table name as one thing.

Back to the point

Facts 2 and 4 together explain the whole session: a model that only sees your prompt, and is trained to answer anyway, will fill every gap with a confident guess. So say the gaps out loud.

Claude now writes a long query. A typical one looks something like this (yours will differ, because the model samples):

A typical best guess · illustrative, not from the warehouse
-- Assumptions: adoption = first purchase in the last 90 days
WITH first_purchase AS (
    SELECT cl.consumer_id, MIN(cl.event_date) AS first_date
    FROM clr.consumer_lifecycle cl          -- invented table
    JOIN ref.product p ON p.product_id = cl.product_id
    WHERE p.product_name = 'VEEV' AND cl.country = 'Indonesia'
    GROUP BY cl.consumer_id
)
SELECT DATE_TRUNC('week', first_date) AS week, COUNT(*) AS adopters
FROM first_purchase
WHERE first_date >= CURRENT_DATE - INTERVAL '90 days'
GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY 1;

3 minutes

If a student asks…

The questions that actually come up, with answers you can say out loud.

"My Claude got it right first time. Did I do something wrong?"

No, you got a good draft. But you only know that because you ran it and read the number. Your neighbour's draft looked just as good and returned zero rows. That's the habit: read it, run it, check it.

"Why does the vague question get questions back instead of an answer?"

Newer models are trained to ask when a request is ambiguous. That's good. But in real life people say "just give me something", and that's when it guesses. That's why Step 1 has a second prompt.

"Why is NULL not just treated as 'not 4'?"

Because in SQL a blank means "unknown", and any comparison with unknown is unknown. "Is an unknown campaign not 4?" Could be, could not; SQL refuses to say yes. WHERE only keeps rows where the answer is yes. IS DISTINCT FROM is the version that treats a blank as an ordinary value.

"I left out the consent rule and still got 17 · 16 · 25. Why?"

Lucky. In Indonesia no under-25 has a "yes" permission slip, so the age-gate and the consent rule remove exactly the same 9 people. In a market where the two rules differ, leaving one out would change the number. Keep both rules.

"Why do the three months add up to more than the quarter?"

Some people adopt in two different months, like trying it in July and converting in September. They count once in each month, but once in the quarter: 67 month-counts, 63 different people.

"The consent table says consent_status = 'granted' but marketing_allowed = false. Which one wins?"

marketing_allowed is the column the business uses to decide. For Indonesian under-25s, the slip may say "granted", but marketing is still not allowed because of the age rule. The lab only uses marketing_allowed.

"Isn't the legal age in Indonesia 21?"

Yes, in real life (since 2024); Japan's is 20. HORIZON is fictional training data and uses one 18-24 band in every market. Noticing that is exactly the judgment the day is about. Praise it.

"Can't Claude just run the query itself?"

Not in the setup today: Claude Desktop has no connection to the warehouse, so you carry every query across and you're the one who checks. Session 4 shows what changes when a model can query the warehouse (and what doesn't).

"Is it OK to paste real data into the prompt to help it?"

No. Table names, column names, definitions and rules: yes. Real consumer rows, passwords, connection strings: never. Everything you paste leaves the company's data boundary. Today's prompts only ever contain names and rules.
